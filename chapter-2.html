Chapter 2 · Looking inside

What's Inside

Take a memristor apart and you find a sandwich: two slices of metal with a very thin filling in the middle. All the magic happens in that filling.

The three layers

The two metal slices are called electrodes. They are just the doors in and out for the electricity, connecting the memristor to the rest of the circuit. The filling is the switching layer. It is usually a metal oxide, which is a metal that has joined with oxygen, a bit like rust.

On its own, the oxide is a poor conductor. Electrons find it very hard to cross. So a brand new memristor is like a hallway with the door almost completely shut.

How thin is thin?

The switching layer is only about 5 to 20 nanometres thick. A nanometre is one billionth of a metre.

A human hair is roughly 80,000 nanometres wide. So you could stack thousands of these layers inside the width of one hair.

Try to picture it

If the switching layer were as thick as a sheet of paper, a human hair would be as wide as a two-storey house.

Where is the door?

In the real device, there is no sliding door. Instead, tiny weak spots inside the oxide can line up to form a bridge from one electrode to the other. When the bridge is complete, electrons can walk across easily. When it is broken, they are stuck. The next chapter shows how that bridge is built and broken.

What are they made of?

Different labs and companies use different recipes, but most memristors you will read about use materials like these:

  • Electrodes: platinum, titanium nitride, titanium, tantalum or copper.
  • Switching layer: hafnium oxide, tantalum oxide or titanium oxide.

Hafnium oxide is a favourite because chip factories already use it inside normal transistors, so they know how to handle it.

Name check

You will often see memristors called ReRAM or RRAM, short for resistive random-access memory. Same device, just named after its most common job.

What we learned

  • A memristor is a metal / oxide / metal sandwich.
  • The metal layers are electrodes. The oxide in the middle is the switching layer.
  • The switching layer is only a few nanometres thick.
  • The “door” is really a tiny bridge that forms and breaks inside the oxide.

Chapter 4 · The famous curve

The Bow-Tie Curve

If you look up memristors, you will see one picture everywhere: a curve shaped like a bow tie. It looks scary, but it is just the story of Chapter 3 drawn as a graph.

What the graph shows

We slowly turn the voltage up, then down, then negative, then back to zero. At every moment we measure how much current flows. Voltage goes along the bottom. Current goes up the side.

For a normal resistor, this graph is just a straight line through the middle: more push, more current, always the same amount. For a memristor, the line splits into two paths, because the door changes while we sweep.

Try it yourself

Walk around the bow tie

Press play and follow the red dot. The numbered steps below light up as it goes.

Voltage: +0.00 VCurrent: +0.00 mADoor: closed (high resistance)
  1. Going up, door closed. The bridge is broken, so the current only creeps up slowly.
  2. SET! At about +1 V the bridge snaps together. The current jumps up, until the compliance limit catches it.
  3. Coming back down, door open. The bridge stays. The current follows a much steeper line.
  4. Going negative, still open. Current now flows backwards, and lots of it.
  5. RESET! At about −1 V the bridge breaks. The current suddenly drops.
  6. Back to zero, door closed. We are back where we started, ready to go round again.

Why is it called “pinched”?

Look at the very middle of the graph. Both paths pass through the exact point where voltage is zero and current is zero. The loop is squeezed shut there, like a bow tie knot.

That makes sense: with no push, no electrons move, whether the door is open or closed. The door’s position is still stored, you just can’t see it until you push.

The official name

Scientists call this shape a pinched hysteresis loop. “Hysteresis” just means the path going up is different from the path coming down, because the device remembers its past.

Reading real graphs

Real measurements look messier than this clean drawing. The jumps are not perfectly sharp, and each loop is a little different from the last. Scientists often draw the current on a log scale so that tiny and huge currents fit on one graph. The shape changes, but the story is always the same: slow, jump up, steep, jump down.

Quick check

In our example, the closed door is 10 kΩ and the open door is 1 kΩ. So at +0.5 V, the current is 0.05 mA when closed and 0.5 mA when open. Same push, ten times the current.

What we learned

  • The bow tie is a graph of current against voltage during a full sweep.
  • The two paths are the door closed (shallow) and door open (steep).
  • The jumps are SET (positive side) and RESET (negative side).
  • Both paths cross at zero, which is why the loop is pinched.

Chapter 6 · Arrays

Grids of Memristors

One memristor stores a bit or two. To store millions, we arrange them in a grid called a crossbar. It is beautifully simple, with one sneaky problem.

The crossbar

Lay some wires side by side as rows. Lay more wires on top, crossing them, as columns. Put a memristor at every place where a row crosses a column. That is a crossbar array.

To reach one memristor, you pick its row and its column, and push voltage between them. No extra parts needed.

Why engineers love it

Each memristor takes up only the space of one wire crossing. That makes crossbars one of the densest ways to pack memory onto a chip. They can even be stacked in layers, like floors in a building.

In chip language, rows are often called word lines and columns are called bit lines.

The sneaky problem: sneak paths

When you push voltage onto a row to read one memristor, the current does not only go where you want. It can also take a detour: across a neighbour, down another column, across another neighbour, and back into your column. This detour is called a sneak path.

If the neighbours happen to be in the low resistance state, a lot of current sneaks through. Now the memristor you are reading looks like it is open even when it is closed. You read a 1 instead of a 0.

Leaky pipes

Imagine checking whether one tap is on by listening at the drain. If all the pipes are connected, water from other taps reaches the drain too, and you cannot tell which tap it came from.

Fixing it: add a gatekeeper

The usual fix is to put a small gatekeeper in front of each memristor, so current can only pass when we choose that exact cell.

  • 1T1R: one transistor, one resistor. A transistor acts as a proper switch. Very reliable, but takes more space.
  • 1S1R: one selector, one resistor. A selector only lets current through when the voltage is big enough, so the small sneak voltages get blocked.

What we learned

  • A crossbar puts a memristor at every crossing of rows and columns.
  • It is very dense, and can be stacked in layers.
  • Current can take a detour through neighbours: a sneak path.
  • Sneak paths cause wrong reads.
  • A transistor (1T1R) or selector (1S1R) in each cell blocks them.

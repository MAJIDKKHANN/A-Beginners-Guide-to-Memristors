Chapter 8 · Security

No Two Are the Same

Every memristor is a tiny bit different, even when they are made side by side on the same chip. That is a headache for memory, but it is a gift for security.

Twins that are not identical

Remember that the bridge forms from oxygen vacancies. Where exactly they line up, how thick the bridge grows and where it breaks is partly random, down to single atoms. So no two memristors are exactly alike. This is called variability.

For memory, variability is a problem: you want every 0 to look the same. But for security, it is exactly what you want.

A fingerprint for a chip

Fake chips are a real problem. Copies can look identical from the outside. How can a device prove it is genuine? One answer is to give every chip a fingerprint that comes from its own tiny random differences, a fingerprint that even the factory could not copy.

This is called a PUF, a physical unclonable function. “Physical” because it comes from the material itself. “Unclonable” because nobody can control atoms precisely enough to make a copy.

Turning differences into bits

A simple way to read the fingerprint: take memristors in pairs and ask “which one has higher resistance?” Left bigger gives a 1, right bigger gives a 0. Nobody chose these bits. They come straight from the atoms.

No secret to steal

A normal chip keeps its secret key stored in memory, where an attacker might dig it out. A PUF does not store the key anywhere. It regenerates it from the physics every time it is needed.

Try it yourself

Make some chips

Each chip has 16 memristors. Darker cells have higher resistance. Pairs are compared to make an 8-bit fingerprint. Build a few chips and see how different they are, then read the same chip again.

The tricky part: noise

Every time you read a memristor, the number wobbles a tiny bit. If two memristors in a pair are almost equal, that wobble can flip which one looks bigger, and a bit changes. A fingerprint that changes is not much use.

Engineers handle this in a few ways: they skip pairs that are too close to call, they read several times and take a vote, and they add error correction, extra maths that can fix a few flipped bits. Getting this right is where most of the real engineering happens.

A note from the author

This chapter is close to my heart. My undergraduate thesis was about building exactly this kind of fingerprint from ReRAM, including a pipeline to correct the bits that flip. It is what got me into memristors in the first place.

What we learned

  • No two memristors are identical: this is variability.
  • For memory it is a problem. For security it is useful.
  • A PUF turns tiny random differences into a chip fingerprint.
  • Comparing pairs of resistances is a simple way to make bits.
  • Read noise can flip bits, so real designs use error correction.

Memristors That Do Math

A crossbar can do more than store numbers. With a little trick, it can multiply and add huge lists of numbers in one go, which is exactly what AI needs.

Multiplying with one memristor

First, a new word. Conductance is the opposite of resistance: how easily current flows. An open door has high conductance. A closed door has low conductance. Conductance is measured in siemens (S), and 1 millisiemens (mS) is the conductance of a 1 kΩ resistor.

This is Ohm’s law. Put a voltage in, and the current that comes out is the voltage multiplied by the conductance. So if we store a number as the memristor’s conductance, and feed another number in as a voltage, the current is their product. No calculator needed.

Adding with a wire

Now put several memristors on the same column. Each one makes its own current. All those currents pour into the same wire, and a wire simply adds up everything that flows into it.

1.0 + 0.6 + 0.2 = 1.8 mA. The column did a whole row of multiplications and the total, all at once, for free.

The official name

Multiplying pairs of numbers and adding the results is called a multiply-accumulate, or MAC. It is the single most common operation in AI.

Why AI cares

A neural network, the kind of AI behind image recognition and chatbots, is mostly a giant pile of multiply-accumulates. It multiplies its inputs by millions of stored numbers called weights, and adds the results.

In a normal computer, those weights live in memory, and every single one has to travel to the processor, get multiplied, and travel back. Moving the data often costs more time and energy than the maths itself. This traffic jam is known as the memory wall.

In a memristor crossbar, the weights are stored as the conductances, right where the maths happens. Nothing travels. You feed in voltages and read out answers. This idea is called compute-in-memory.

The honest catch

Memristor conductances are a bit noisy, and converting between voltages and digital numbers takes extra circuits. So these chips are great for AI that can tolerate small errors, and still an active research area.

What we learned

  • Conductance is how easily current flows, the opposite of resistance.
  • One memristor multiplies: current = voltage × conductance.
  • A column wire adds all the currents flowing into it.
  • A crossbar does a whole set of multiply-accumulates in one step.
  • Storing AI weights as conductances avoids moving data: compute-in-memory.

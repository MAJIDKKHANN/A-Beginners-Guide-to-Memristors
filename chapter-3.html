Chapter 3 · How it switches

Building a Tiny Bridge

Inside the oxide, a tiny bridge can be built and broken. Building it is called SET. Breaking it is called RESET. That is all switching really is.

Stepping stones across a river

Picture the oxide as a river, and the electrons as people who want to cross it. They cannot swim. But the river has stepping stones scattered around. If enough stones line up in a row from one bank to the other, people can walk right across.

In a real memristor, the stepping stones are spots where an oxygen atom is missing from the oxide. Scientists call them oxygen vacancies. Electrons can hop from one vacancy to the next. A line of them from the top electrode to the bottom one is called a filament, the bridge in our story.

Step 1: Forming, the very first bridge

A brand new memristor has no bridge at all. To build the first one, we give it a bigger push than usual, a voltage of a few volts. This pulls oxygen out of place and lines up the first row of stepping stones. This one-time step is called forming.

Only once

Forming happens one time, at the start of a memristor’s life. After that, it only ever needs smaller pushes.

Step 2 and 3: SET and RESET

RESET means pushing current backwards. This pulls a few stones out of the top of the bridge and leaves a gap. The bridge is broken, electrons get stuck, and the resistance jumps up.

SET means pushing current forwards again. The gap fills back in, the bridge is complete, and the resistance drops down.

Because only a tiny gap has to open and close, this can happen over and over, many thousands of times or more.

Two words to remember

LRS: low resistance state. Bridge complete, door open.

HRS: high resistance state. Bridge broken, door closed.

A safety limit

When SET completes the bridge, current suddenly rushes through. If nothing stops it, the bridge can grow too thick, and then RESET struggles to break it. So circuits add a compliance current: a maximum current the memristor is allowed during SET. Think of it as a guard at the bridge who only lets so many people on at once.

Not always vacancies

Some memristors build their bridge from metal atoms instead, usually copper or silver that drifts in from an electrode. The idea is the same: a bridge that forms and breaks.

What we learned

  • The bridge is made of oxygen vacancies, spots where oxygen is missing.
  • A complete bridge is called a filament.
  • Forming builds the first bridge, once. SET completes it, RESET breaks it.
  • Complete bridge = LRS. Broken bridge = HRS.
  • A compliance current stops the bridge growing too thick.
